First and foremost, I would like to extend my sincere gratitude to the friend who recommended this book to me. Throughout my reading, a whirlwind of emotions surged within—at times dynamic and turbulent, at other times gentle and serene.

As it happens, I have encountered many stories revolving around the era of the March 1st Movement over the past few years. From the biography of Baekbeom Kim Koo that I recently revisited to novels like *Beasts of a Little Land*, we have grown up immersed in writings that highlight the historical significance and noble spirit of the independence movement. Because of this, such narratives often feel natural, almost second nature to us.

As I have noted in earlier writings, while central figures inevitably lead the overarching narrative, there are always countless supporting characters surrounding them—each carrying their own distinct, lived story. I have always found myself drawn to these narratives of people on the periphery.

Even as he had to navigate involuntary choices forced upon him by a turbulent era, the author captures the innocence of his childhood and his quiet impressions as an individual living through an epochal transition with remarkable restraint. The writing is truly exquisite.

Yet, the fact that such a beautiful work of literature was celebrated and featured in the school textbooks of a foreign country leaves me, as a fellow Korean citizen, with a profoundly bittersweet, almost desolate feeling. While modern Korea faces no shortage of daunting challenges, I deeply wish that gentle, everyday literary gems like this could reach more of our children and contemporaries. At the same time, I cannot help but feel a lingering sense of despair, wondering just how many people in today's Korea still possess the sensitivity to understand and cherish the profound value of such sentences.
