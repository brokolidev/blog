Since my Google purchase history doesn't go back far enough cleanly, I can't recall the exact date I bought this book, but I remember talking about it with my mother-in-law around the time I got married—so it must have been at least eight or nine years ago. I probably tucked the title away in the back of my mind after hearing the news of the Man Booker International Prize and bought it shortly after, which matches that timeline just about right. Rereading this story after nearly a decade was quite a shock. You would think a story this jarring would leave some trace in memory no matter how much time had passed, yet I only faintly remembered the first chapter, "The Vegetarian," while the later chapters read as grippingly as if I were encountering them for the very first time. Even now, I'm not particularly fond of short fiction, so the rest of the book felt so new to me that I half-wondered whether I had simply put the book down after the first chapter back then—and thanks(?) to that, I was able to read it again with fresh eyes. There are many pleasures in revisiting a work you enjoyed in the past after time has flowed by. Because of my habit of avoiding author's notes or critical commentary whenever possible and stubbornly trying to make sense of a work in my own unrefined way, even the exact same book can feel completely different depending on my circumstances, environment, and state of mind. If it were a cherished personal memory, digging it up and reshaping it might be unwelcome; when it comes to appreciating music, books, or films, however, even if the unearthed past turns out rougher than expected, it does no harm—and more often than not, it actually helps. That is why I find myself rereading or rewatching things whenever they cross my mind like this.

After finishing Han Kang's *Black Deer*, I wanted to go straight into rereading *The Vegetarian* before the lingering resonance faded. Perhaps because of that, it was both welcoming and fascinating to notice the shared emotional currents running through both works. While I don't usually gravitate toward standalone short stories, a linked novel like this is something I find all the more compelling. Even when people share the exact same event, how it is understood varies wildly depending on whose eyes you look through—and in a way, that couldn't be more natural. Sometimes, even while reading a novel from the protagonist's viewpoint, I try putting myself in the shoes of a passing minor character; this book records emotions from a different character's perspective in each chapter, giving the narrative a striking three-dimensional depth. When I come across memorable lines while reading, I often highlight them, and I'd like to jot down a few passages here.

> Perhaps inside her, something truly terrible—events no one could ever imagine—was taking place, and simply carrying on with daily life alongside it was already more than she could bear. Perhaps that was why she had no energy left over to feel curiosity, to explore, or to react to every little thing in everyday life.
> — Han Kang, *The Vegetarian* (p. 137), Kindle Edition.

> Could it not have been stopped—all those things no one could ever fathom seeping into Yeong-hye's very bones?
> — Han Kang, *The Vegetarian* (p. 252), Kindle Edition.

> It was true. She had never really lived. From as far back in childhood as she could remember, she had merely endured.
> — Han Kang, *The Vegetarian* (p. 259), Kindle Edition.

> Even after everything has passed, even after living through such horrific things, people still eat and drink, relieve themselves, wash their bodies, and go on living. Sometimes they even laugh out loud.
> — Han Kang, *The Vegetarian* (p. 270), Kindle Edition.

Characters living with an inner world too heavy to bear—just like those in *Black Deer*—are depicted repeatedly and revealed in varied ways throughout this book as well. The descriptions are so piercingly raw that one wonders how the author could possibly inhabit such emotions and put them into sentences without having lived through those dreadful memories herself. I also found myself imagining what kind of world *The Vegetarian* portrays if we understand its characters not as literal individuals, but as figurative metaphors. Sadly, human beings do not seem to have changed all that much between the time the author conceived and unfolded this book and today. And that is why I must try harder. I must not forget, and I must keep doing what I can without growing weary.
